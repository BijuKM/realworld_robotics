Realworld Robotics

Generative AI — especially LLMs and multimodal models — has improved exponentially in the past year and automated practically anything software-related. So it begs the question: can some of those benefits spill over into activities beyond the confines of the computer or the virtual world?

From my limited experience playing with AI on edge devices, I'd say the time is ripe to explore whether robots actually have what it takes — or, if not today, whether they can be enhanced to work independently in the real world, with all its randomness and chaos. Can they deliver a real return on the investment in cost and effort? And can it be done at a reasonable price?

This is a project in that direction. Or rather — it's not a single project so much as a set of projects, each providing one or more of the foundational blocks, that ultimately culminate in an umbrella project delivering autonomous, genuinely useful behavior in a public environment.

Project Requirements

This project (or, I guess, these projects) is a hobbyist endeavour, so it needs to stay within a hobbyist's budget — standard, off-the-shelf, inexpensive components. And if something's just out of reach, can we improvise by cobbling together a few cheaper options to do the job?

So, how do we even go about figuring out the requirements? One way is to look at an analogy: a toddler who has just picked up enough motor skills and language to wander to any corner of the room — or the house, really — and carry out simple commands like "open the door" or "pick that up." You'd expect the kid to dodge obstacles and find a way around them. And if the instruction is confusing? They look up and ask, "What do you mean?"

For a start, let's aim to have our robot do the same.

So let's lock in these requirements — at least for now. We'll keep adding to the list as we go.

  1. Receive verbal commands, act on them if it can, and respond verbally.
  2. Be polite. Socially acceptable conversation skills would be great, but at minimum, "please" and "sorry" are non-negotiable.
  3. Navigate to any point in the room, building, or locality on its own, working around whatever's in the way.
  4. Maintain a collision zone and stop any physical action if a person or object encroaches on it.
  5. Visually recognize objects.
  6. Pick up objects flexibly — regardless of shape, position, orientation, or fragility.
  7. Track where things are (position and orientation) and remember it for later, so things can be put back where they belong.
  8. Nice to have: watch a human do a small task and replicate it.

Foundational Blocks

The requirements we locked in above give us the building blocks we need to design around:

1) Voice-based Interaction

Fortunately, the improvements in multimodal models hand us almost everything we need here. It's mostly a matter of wiring the pieces together.Text-to-Speech and Speech-to-Text models are light enough to run in real time on standard hardware like a Raspberry Pi. They convert human speech to text and back.

For simple commands like "turn left" or "pick up X", the text output can go straight to the motors and actuators. But for something more complex — say,"fetch the newspaper"— that command needs to be fed into the multimodal model, which breaks it down into smaller steps: navigate to the door, open the door, pick up the newspaper, close the door, and so on.

The multimodal model can also handle general chat, answer questions about the environment, and adopt a persona to keep things conversational (shoutout to requirement 2).

One note: I'd strongly prefer to run the LLM locally on a dedicated accelerator rather than lean on an internet connection. I'm using the Axera LLM-8850 (8 GB), which seems to be out of stock at the moment. Also, if we can get interaction working over a Bluetooth headset, that'd be ideal — no need to shout commands while the robot wanders out of hearing range.

2) Mobility

This is where we give the robot the ability to actually move around. We can start simple — a differential-wheeled setup with two drive wheels and a caster — and work our way up to advanced options like a two-legged platform or even something aerial (drone-style). The latter becomes an absolute must for vertical travel: negotiating floors, escalators, and the like.

Even within the wheeled camp, we can step up from a two-wheel differential drive to a four-wheeled configuration once we're lugging heavier payloads, say, a robotic arm.These robots are controlled by feeding them a forward/backward velocity and a turn rate. A proper PID loop is essential to make sure the actual velocity and angle match what we asked for — and that means drive motors with encoders for feedback.

The initial version of our robot will be built using an Arduino controlling the motors via an LM298 H-bridge driver, with the Arduino itself fed the velocity and turn by the Raspberry Pi.

3) Object Recognition

This isn't really a standalone block so much as a capability the other blocks depend on. In the voice-interaction block, the robot needs to "recognize" what a command means. During navigation, it needs to identify an object — a door, say — before heading toward it. For picking tasks, it needs to recognize the target object and its position and orientation.

This is where most of our effort will go, because there's no single method that fits everywhere. From what I've dug into so far, I think we have a handful of approaches to mix and match:

  1. Computer vision - mainly OpenCV for image recognition. Fastest option, provided we can find reliable visual features to key off.
  2. Machine learning — the YOLO family, again for image recognition. Needs a training pass for each specific object. YOLOE is a nice middle ground: you can train on a single image, though the detection rate may not hit the mark.
  3. Multimodal GenAI models — for both image and voice recognition. Slowest of the bunch, but also the most likely to just... get it right.
  4. Depth sensing — laser or ultrasonic time-of-flight sensors to grab depth info and tell the difference between a real object and a picture of one

We'll be layering all of these across the projects.

4) Navigation

Think of this as "get the robot to a specific spot.".

In the real world, a person would say "go near the kitchen table" or "stop about three feet to the left of the door," not "travel to coordinates x, y" — which is how most robotics frameworks handle it.

Fortunately, there are libraries that map a room's layout using LiDAR and others that compute a path to a target point while avoiding obstacles automatically. The catch? The resulting map only records distance to an obstacle — a wall, a door — but not what that obstacle is. Identifying the object requires visual input from a camera, so we'll need to merge LiDAR and vision data to navigate anything that isn't a sterile lab.

Another wrinkle: most off-the-shelf LiDARs are 2-D. They scan a single height plane, and anything above or below that plane is invisible to them. Fine for a vacuum cleaner. Not fine for the messy, three-dimensional world we actually live in. We'll need 3-D LiDAR, and since those are eye-wateringly expensive, I suspect we'll be improvising our own from cheaper parts.

5) Arm & Gripper

I'm going to start with the commonly available 6-DOF metallic robotic arms — they handle heavier loads — but that comes with the baggage of extra power requirements and a lot of balancing.

As noted in the requirements, the gripper needs to grab anything, regardless of shape or orientation. The plan: use a camera mounted on the arm, trace the object's outline with OpenCV, and drive the gripper fingers to match.

We'll also need tactile feedback sensors in the fingers so the robot doesn't crush whatever it's holding. And the standard two-finger gripper will need at least one extra finger underneath to cradle fragile objects and stop them slipping through when the grip is necessarily light.

What is ROS 2 and why should we use it?

The Robot Operating System, version 2 — which, despite the name, is not an operating system at all. It's a framework that binds sensors, actuators, and robotic components together into a functioning whole.

Think of it as the hub of a wheel: each spoke connects to a specific component — differential drive, cameras, LiDAR, arm — and they all talk through that central hub. It provides the communication glue and ships out-of-the-box drivers for most common components. A manufacturer just needs to ensure their hardware speaks the ROS2 protocol.

That last part is huge for us. Swap out a sensor or a motor, and we're not reinventing the wheel every time. On top of that, it bundles libraries for the heavy lifting — SLAM, path planning, the works — so we can focus on the fun bits rather than the plumbing.

And Finally, the Projects

As they say, necessity is the mother of invention. So what better way to pick a project than to point the robot(s) at a real, everyday pain point? The project is a success if the pain point actually goes away.

I'll be tackling a couple of home projects, mostly so that any collateral damage stays, well, in-house. And even though each project will be aiming at a specific goal, the real goal is generating reusable building blocks. So the solutions should stay as generic and extensible as possible.

After a day of DIY, my work area is usually a disaster. The floor is a graveyard of sawdust, iron filings, wood shavings, plastic dust, crumpled paper, cardboard, packaging, hand tools, power tools — the full catalogue. A huge chunk of my evening is just cleaning up and sorting through that mess. It would help me no end if a robot could take that off my hands.

So I'm splitting it into two projects: one to pick up and stow anything useful, and the other to sweep up the rest.

Home Project 1 — An Autonomous Sweeper Robot

Check out Autonomous Sweeper Robot for details

Once I've cleared the "preservables" off the floor, my trusty broom — with myself at the other end — usually handles the rest. The vacuum cleaner is, frankly, too delicate to expose to this kind of chaos.

The goal here: remove both the broom and me from the equation. The robot should be able to sweep all debris from a given area into a dustpan that might be sitting anywhere in the room.

The "given area" may be either:

  • The entire sweepable area of the room — where we lean on LiDAR, room-mapping (like SLAM_Toolbox) and navigation (for example, the NAV2 stack) — or
  • Just the patch flagged by an automatic debris-detection sweep (we'll be tracking debris with a combination of laser beams and OpenCV).

The dustpan's location will need to be re-identified every run — YOLO-E on the camera feed should be more than enough for that.

Other building blocks that'll get built here (and will likely carry over into the other projects):
  1. Two-wheel differential drive with PID control
  2. Text-to-Speech output over a Bluetooth headset

Home Project 2 — A Tool Organising Robot

Before the sweeper can do its thing, we need to get the important stuff — tools, components, the like — off the floor. That's what this project is for.

To start with, I'll use OpenCV to detect objects with non-random outlines (i.e. anything that some decipherable shape) and have the robot place them in a designated tray. Down the line, YOLO can categorise them into tools, components, whatever. The holy grail, though? Picking up a tiny resistor, reading its colour bands, and filing it in the right bin. That's the dream.

This is where we'll build out the robotic arm block.

The Main Project — An Autonomous Library Assistant

This is the one meant for the real world. The deployment target is a public library — busy enough to be interesting, with plenty of people milling about, but not quite as chaotic as a train platform at rush hour.

A library is also a gift in terms of structure: shelves are properly tagged with text, barcodes, QR codes — all of which are now machine-readable. That lets us validate operations end-to-end. Because for all its improvements, AI is still very much prone to hallucinations, and "I'm pretty sure that's Pride and Prejudice" isn't exactly a shelf you can point a robot at.

Here's what it'd need to do, from both sides of the counter:

PerspectiveExpectations
Administrative 1) Transport returned books to the correct shelf and slot
2) Rearrange books that have drifted out of order
3) Pick up and shelve books that are just... lying around
4) Cross-reference the physical stack with the catalogue database
User 1) Answer queries about availability and shelf location
2) Fetch a requested book off the shelf, if it's there
3) Suggest alternatives if the requested title is unavailable
4) Walk through the borrowing process on the user's behalf
The two home projects should give us most of the technical groundwork — the mobility, the vision, the arm, the voice — that this one needs. The library project is really where all those building blocks get pulled together into something that's useful, visible, and (hopefully) polite.
